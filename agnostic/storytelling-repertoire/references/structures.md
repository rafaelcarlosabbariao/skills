# Structures: the arc of a piece and the shape of an argument

Checked by web search, 2026-11-18. Each entry: what it is, when it fits a research report or deck, when it fails.

## Argument structures

### Minto's pyramid and SCQA
- **What:** Barbara Minto, a former McKinsey consultant, *The Pyramid Principle*. Situation, complication,
  question, answer opens the piece; the answer sits on top and the supporting arguments group beneath it. Edition
  dates vary across sources (1987, 1996, 2010 and later are all cited); cite the edition in hand.
- **Fits:** LinkedIn posts, a commission summary, the first paragraph a decider reads. The reader gets the answer
  before the evidence.
- **Fails:** A report whose finding needs the reader to see the evidence first, or whose point is a question left
  open. Answer-first turns a proposition into a verdict, which the scaffold forbids.

### Duarte's sparkline (*Resonate*, 2010)
- **What:** Nancy Duarte maps a talk as movement between "what is" and "what could be"; she drew the contour of
  King's "I Have a Dream" and of a Feynman lecture. ("New bliss" for the end state appears only in secondary notes.)
- **Fits:** The scaffold (step 6) and any deck that ends on a proposal. Alternating the record with the possibility
  keeps a proposal from reading as an instruction.
- **Fails:** When "what could be" is a pitch. The scaffold is a proposition the reader may decline.

### ABT, and but therefore (Randy Olson, *Houston, We Have a Narrative*, University of Chicago Press, 2015)
- **What:** Momentum (and), conflict (but), resolution (therefore). Olson credits the South Park writers' habit of
  replacing "and" with "but" or "therefore".
- **Fits:** The one-sentence test for every finding, slide and nut graf. A deck's claims read in order should make
  one ABT.
- **Fails:** As a template filled mechanically ("but" with no real tension).

### The story spine (Kenn Adams, late 1980s; Pixar via Emma Coats's 2011 list, rule 4)
- **What:** Once upon a time; every day; one day; because of that (repeated); until finally; ever since then.
  Wording and length (seven or eight lines) vary by source.
- **Fits:** Then-and-now histories: a product's first year, a move from one format to another, a precedent told as an event.
- **Fails:** On findings with no sequence; it forces a plot onto a cross-section.

### Freytag's pyramid (Gustav Freytag, *Die Technik des Dramas*, 1863)
- **What:** Exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, dénouement, drawn from Greek and Shakespearean drama.
- **Fits:** The long report: the question and its human scene (exposition), observations that build (rising), one
  turn, the limits (falling), the scaffold.
- **Fails:** On a slide; it is an arc for a piece, too long for one claim.

### The six-page narrative memo (Amazon; Bezos's email of 9 June 2004, restated in the 2017 shareholder letter)
- **What:** A narrative document read in silence at the start of a meeting, in place of slides driving the
  discussion. Accounts call it a ban on PowerPoint; it is a rule for executive meetings.
- **Fits:** Treating the slide set as the shareable cut of a long page. The report is the document; the
  deck follows it. Tufte argues the same case (below).
- **Fails:** When the long page is a slide deck in paragraphs.

### The case method (Harvard Business School; Dean Wallace Donham from 1919; Melvin Copeland's *Marketing Problems*, 1920; "case system" voted 1922)
- **What:** The reader stands in the protagonist's position with the facts and a decision to make, and no answer
  given.
- **Fits:** The scaffold and commission conversations: lay out the gap and the options, let the reader decide.
- **Fails:** When the facts are selected to force one answer.

## Narrative craft

### Thick description (Clifford Geertz, *The Interpretation of Cultures*, 1973; after Gilbert Ryle's wink and twitch)
- **What:** Description that carries the meaning of an act, so a wink is told apart from a twitch. "Deep Play:
  Notes on the Balinese Cockfight" (first published 1972) reads one event as the society's commentary on itself.
- **Fits:** Step 1's human scene and each observation's story: an event, a prompt, a room.
- **Fails:** Critics note "Deep Play" gives no blow-by-blow account of a fight; a scene must still be shown, and
  its claim held to the counts.

### The anecdotal lede and nut graf (the "Wall Street Journal formula"; Scanlan, Poynter, 2003)
- **What:** A scene or person first, then the paragraph that says what the story is about and why to keep
  reading, then the evidence, often returning to the scene at the end.
- **Fits:** Report openings and Substack posts. For a study, the key finding goes in the nut graf (NPR training).
- **Fails:** A lede that runs long before the reader learns the subject.

### Anecdote, then reflection (Ira Glass, *This American Life*)
- **What:** One moment after another, then a moment that says why it matters, alternating.
- **Fits:** Each observation in step 4: the event at one level, then the mechanism line.
- **Fails:** Reflection with no event, which reads as teaching.

### The information gap and the reveal (George Loewenstein, "The Psychology of Curiosity", *Psychological Bulletin* 116(1), 1994)
- **What:** Curiosity rises when the reader notices a gap in what they know, and peaks when they have a good guess
  but are unsure.
- **Fits:** Asking the reader to predict before showing the answer; a question as the cover title (step 1).
- **Fails:** When the gap is too wide to guess at, or the answer confirms the guess (a reveal with nothing in it).

### One person standing for many, and its risk
- **What:** The identifiable-victim effect, first described by Thomas Schelling (1968). Small, Loewenstein and
  Slovic (2007) found donations highest for one identified victim, and that adding statistics reduced them. A
  pre-registered replication (Maier and colleagues) found no support with hypothetical donations, and a 2016
  meta-analysis found the effect small.
- **Fits:** A room or an object standing for a group, placed visibly inside the distribution it comes from.
- **Fails:** When the one case is read as typical. With a counts-only rule for social corpora (no user comment
  quoted), the case is a room, an object or a public record.

### Precedent as rhyme
- **What:** Setting a past case beside the present with the difference named. "History does not repeat itself, but
  it rhymes" is attributed to Mark Twain with no supporting evidence; its earliest print appearance found is 1970
  (Quote Investigator). Do not attribute it to Twain.
- **Fits:** Step 2 (precedent) and step 6 (the past).
- **Fails:** Analogy with the difference left out.

### Concreteness and the unexpected (Chip and Dan Heath, *Made to Stick*, 2007)
- **What:** Six traits of ideas that last: simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional, stories.
- **Fits:** Choosing units ("hours a year with friends", not "time use") and openings.
- **Fails:** As a checklist applied after the finding is set.

## Presentation as its own research question

- Kosara and Mackinlay, "Storytelling: The Next Step for Visualization", *IEEE Computer* 46(5), 2013: a story is an
  ordered sequence of steps with a clear path; presentation deserves as much care as analysis.
- Hullman and Diakopoulos, "Visualization Rhetoric: Framing Effects in Narrative Visualization", *IEEE TVCG* 17(12),
  2011: annotations, omissions and interaction choices frame the reading. Theory notes on charts are framing;
  name the source and what was left out.
- Segel and Heer, "Narrative Visualization: Telling Stories with Data", *IEEE TVCG* (InfoVis 2010), 58 cases: seven
  genres (magazine style, annotated chart, partitioned poster, flow chart, comic strip, slide show, film or video or
  animation), an author-driven to reader-driven spectrum, and three hybrid structures: martini glass,
  interactive slideshow, drill-down story.
