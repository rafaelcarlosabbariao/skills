# Social Simulation and Complexity

This shelf runs from von Neumann's 29-state grid to LLM agent societies. It exists because three common cultural-analysis instruments are all simulation instruments in disguise. An audience simulation turns an audience into rule sets and runs agents. A forecast ensemble runs many forward simulations. A daily trend read sees emergent macro patterns and has to decide, under time pressure, what kind of process generated them. Every work here answers some version of one question: what can local rules, run forward, tell you about collective behavior, and when should you refuse to believe the output.

## The unified mental model

**1. Local rules grow macro worlds nobody intended.** This is the shelf's spine, and it accumulates in historical order. Von Neumann proved self-reproduction is a logical problem solvable with a finite rule set on a grid, and that the behavior lives in the configuration, not the components. Wolfram showed a one-line rule (rule 30) generates output that passes randomness tests, and a near-trivial rule (rule 110) can compute anything computable. Schelling grew near-total segregation from agents content to be a local minority, by hand, with coins. Epstein and Axtell grew skewed wealth, tribes, trade, and war on Sugarscape from uniform endowments and a handful of movement rules. Park et al. grew information diffusion and an unscripted party from 25 LLM agents with memory and reflection. The practice implication is Epstein and Axtell's hard test: if you cannot state a cultural explanation as agent-level rules that generate the observed curve, it is narrative, not explanation. Demote it in the read.

**2. Macro cannot be read off micro, in either direction.** Schelling's central lesson, in both the 1971 paper and the 1978 book. A 90 percent sorted scene is compatible with a population that mostly wanted mixing. Skewed attention is compatible with uniform underlying merit (Sugarscape grows Pareto tails from uniform draws). The reverse also fails: polling motives does not predict outcomes when behavior is contingent on what others do. This is why trend reads are condition-first: name the sorting condition, never the inferred intent. And it is why audience simulation output is never read back as member intent.

**3. Some systems can only be run, not solved.** Wolfram's computational irreducibility: for a large class of systems there is no shortcut, the fastest route to the future state is running the system. Mitchell's sensitive dependence: even known deterministic rules cap the forecast horizon, because input error doubles and compounds. Together they are the epistemology under ensemble forecasting. Class 1 and 2 regimes (stable, cyclic) permit trend extrapolation. Class 3 (chaotic churn) permits only distributions and condition words. Class 4 (discrete memes and factions colliding) is where simulation buys real lead time. Report the regime alongside the forecast, and refuse point numbers past the horizon.

**4. Threshold distributions decide outcomes, not average preferences.** Schelling's tolerance schedules, critical mass diagrams, and tipping points; Page's threshold and contagion models; Granovetter via Page. Whether a cascade runs is set by the shape of the threshold distribution, especially its sensitive tail, not by the mean. A scene can sit near an unstable equilibrium where small shocks decide everything (the dying seminar). Distribution shape is also a diagnosis of process: normal tails mean many independent additive causes, power-law tails mean feedback, usually preferential attachment. Check the tail before trusting any average.

**5. Ensembles beat single models, and the spread is the answer.** Page proves it: crowd squared error equals average model error minus prediction diversity, so diversity counts one for one with accuracy. Mitchell's chaos argument and Wolfram's irreducibility make the single confident model dishonest anyway. Equifinality (Epstein and Axtell) closes the loop: distinct rule sets grow the same macro pattern, so a sim that matches the data has identified a regime, not the one true mechanism. Run at least three structurally different models, hold claims loosely, and treat disagreement between good models as information about the phenomenon.

**6. LLMs are populations, and the audit is the method.** The four modern papers form one argument. Park: the architecture (memory stream, retrieval, reflection, planning), not the raw model, is what makes long-horizon believable agents, and emergence claims require ablation. Argyle: model bias is demographically patterned, so conditioning on rich backstories turns it into subgroup signal, provided the four-criteria fidelity audit passes per task and per domain. Aher: a synthetic panel earns trust only by first replicating a known human result, and fidelity to human error is part of fidelity. Bisbee: the skeptic's gate. Means match, but variance compresses, regression structure breaks, minorities get erased, and outputs drift across prompt phrasings and model versions. The shelf's synthesis: silicon panels are hypothesis generators and pilots, gated by three audits (four criteria, three moments, known-result replication), and never measurement.

## Where the works disagree

**Argyle versus Bisbee is the live wire.** Same method, silicon sampling, opposite verdicts. The reconciliation is in what each tested. Argyle validated marginals and pattern correspondence on subgroups with strong identity anchors, and even there flagged the pure-independent failure. Bisbee tested the second moment and the covariance structure and found failure exactly where the cultural read matters most: spread, subgroup regressions, and the atypical member (the conservative Black voter, the pro-union Republican). Working rule: trust a silicon read in proportion to the strength of the segment's identity anchors, and never for variance, statistical significance, or minority voices. Those require human signal.

**Aher complicates both sides.** Scale improves social fidelity (ultimatum, garden path, Milgram all replicate better in larger models) while alignment tuning injects hyper-accuracy distortion. So a newer, better-aligned model can be a worse crowd simulator. Do not assume the latest model is the best silicon panel; test error realism explicitly.

**Wolfram versus Mitchell on grand theory.** Wolfram claims a sweeping principle (computational equivalence, universality everywhere). Mitchell insists there is no single measure of complexity, roughly forty candidates disagree, and the choice of measure is a modeling decision you must defend. Use Wolfram's four classes as regime language; use Mitchell's pluralism to defend every metric choice, especially the entropy-versus-logical-depth split that the slop detector runs on.

**Epstein and Axtell's hard bar versus Page's pluralism.** Growing Artificial Societies says explanation equals generation, full stop. Page's REDCAPE says models legitimately serve seven different uses and a model good for one can be wrong for another. Resolution: the generative bar applies to explanation claims specifically. Name the use before choosing the model, and only demand growth when the claim is "this mechanism caused that pattern."

**Schelling's minimalism versus Park's heavy architecture.** Schelling got segregation from a threshold and a movement rule; Park needed memory, retrieval, reflection, and recursive planning. Both are right at their horizons. Sweep the two or three load-bearing parameters (threshold F, vacancy rate) before adding psychology, but once a simulation runs past a few dozen interactions, the Park components stop being optional: without reflection the run measures drift, not culture.

## When each work is the right tool

Use von Neumann for replication questions: does this format carry its own build instructions, or is it a screenshot of a screenshot that will decay? Use Wolfram to classify the regime before choosing a forecast posture, and to check whether apparent randomness is generated internally by platform rules. Use Mitchell for measurement choices and network topology reads (gamma, clustering versus path length, hub intervention). Use Schelling 1971 when one specific scene is sorting or tipping; use Schelling 1978 for the wider toolkit: critical mass, lemons markets, coordination traps, musical-chairs identities. Use Epstein and Axtell as the validation standard for any audience sim and as the antidote to merit stories about skew. Use Page whenever a forecast, an adoption curve, or an ensemble is on the table. Use Park to build agents, Argyle to build silicon panels, Aher to validate them, and Bisbee to know when to refuse them.
