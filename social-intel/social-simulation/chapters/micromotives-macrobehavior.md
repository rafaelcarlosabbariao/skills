# Micromotives and Macrobehavior

## The claim

Aggregate social patterns are not scaled-up copies of individual intentions. When each person responds to an environment made of other people's choices, mild local preferences can compound into extreme collective outcomes nobody wanted. You cannot read micro motives off macro behavior, and you cannot predict macro behavior by polling motives: you have to model the interaction.

## Core claims

- Macro outcomes routinely decouple from micro intent. Near-total segregation can emerge from people who would each be content as a minority.
- Contingent behavior, behavior that depends on how many others are doing it, creates equilibria, tipping points, and multiple stable states. Which basin the system sits in matters more than the trend line.
- Some social "findings" are accounting identities. Closed-system constraints (the mathematics of musical chairs) make certain patterns arithmetically necessary, not behavioral news.
- Equilibrium is not endorsement. A stable state can be stably bad; how it holds together is a separate question from whether anyone likes it.
- When everyone prefers the cooperative outcome but no one can afford to move first, the fix is a binding rule or coordination device, not persuasion.

## Frameworks and models

**Checkerboard segregation model.** Two agent types on a grid. Each agent has a tolerance rule, for example: stay if at least one third of my immediate neighbors are my own type, otherwise move to the nearest satisfying open cell. Even with that mild demand, movements cascade and the board sorts into stark homogeneous blocks. First published in Dynamic Models of Segregation (Journal of Mathematical Sociology, 1971), it is the ancestor of every agent-based audience model. The load-bearing parameter is the neighborhood demand fraction and its distribution across agents.

**Critical mass and tipping.** Each person has a threshold: the share of others already doing X before they will do X. Plot the cumulative distribution of thresholds against the 45 degree line. Crossings from above are stable equilibria; crossings from below are unstable tipping points. A scene can have one stable equilibrium near zero and another near saturation, with a critical mass point between. The dying seminar is the canonical case: attendance drifts below critical mass, then unravels. Schelling took "tipping" from Morton Grodzins' neighborhood work and generalized it.

**The lemons family (via Akerlof).** When buyers cannot verify quality, low quality drives out high and the market can collapse entirely. Adverse selection is a self-reinforcing sorting process, not a one-off deception.

**Binary choices with externalities.** Draw two payoff curves, one per choice, each a function of how many others choose R. The crossings define the game: multi-person prisoner's dilemma, coordination, or hybrid. Key parameter k: the minimum coalition size at which cooperating becomes self-sustaining. Hockey players individually skate without helmets, all prefer a universal helmet rule, and only the rule gets them there. Daylight saving is pure coordination: the equilibrium chosen is nearly arbitrary, being on one together is everything.

**Bounded-neighborhood model.** A shared space with a distribution of tolerances instead of a grid. Entry and exit of one type shifts the ratio, which triggers the next-most-sensitive members, which shifts the ratio again. Whether mixing survives depends on the shape of the tolerance distribution, not its mean.

## Decision rules

- If a macro pattern looks extreme (total sorting, sudden collapse, a monoculture feed), do not infer extreme preferences. Model mild preferences plus interaction first; escalate the motive story only if the mild model fails.
- If a behavior is contingent on adoption by others, map the threshold distribution and locate the equilibria before forecasting. Report which side of critical mass the thing sits on, not a trend line.
- If a scene, platform, or norm is fading, check for the dying seminar: near an unstable equilibrium small shocks decide everything, so widen the forecast ensemble there.
- If quality is unverifiable in a market, expect lemons dynamics and watch for the counter-move: signaling, certification, provenance marks.
- If everyone in a system complains but nobody deviates, diagnose a multi-person prisoner's dilemma and look for the binding rule, not the persuasion campaign.
- Before claiming a behavioral shift, run the musical-chairs check: is this a choice pattern or a closed-system identity?

## Key terms

- **Contingent behavior:** behavior whose payoff depends on how many others are doing it.
- **Threshold:** the adoption level at which a given individual joins in.
- **Critical mass:** the adoption level above which a process becomes self-sustaining.
- **Tipping:** the runaway sorting cascade once a ratio crosses the sensitive part of a tolerance distribution.
- **Multiple equilibria:** distinct stable states available to one system; history and shocks pick among them.
- **Multi-person prisoner's dilemma:** all prefer universal cooperation, each prefers to defect unilaterally.
- **Lemons market:** unverifiable quality lets bad goods drive out good through adverse selection.
- **Musical-chairs mathematics:** closed-system identities that make some aggregate patterns necessary, not behavioral.
