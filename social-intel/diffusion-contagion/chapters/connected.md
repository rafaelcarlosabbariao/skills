# Connected

## The claim

Behaviors, emotions, and norms spread person to person through social networks the way pathogens do, and the reach of that influence is bounded: you are shaped by your friends, their friends, and their friends' friends, with the effect decaying to noise beyond three hops. Networks are not just pipes; their structure (who is tied to whom, and where you sit) determines what flows and how far.

## Core claims

- Influence is hyperdyadic. What your friend's friend's friend does measurably changes your odds of doing it, even though you have never met them.
- The Three Degrees of Influence Rule: effects propagate to roughly three degrees of separation, then fade, because signal degrades with retransmission and ties are unstable at long chains.
- Concrete contagions documented in the Framingham Heart Study network (12,067 people tracked 1971-2003): obesity, smoking cessation, happiness, and loneliness all cluster and spread along ties, not just co-occur.
- Network position is fate. Central people catch what flows sooner; peripheral people are buffered but isolated. Smokers and lonely people drifted to the network's edge over time, which compounds the condition.
- Clustering has three rival explanations: contagion (induction), homophily (like chooses like), and shared environment (confounding). Only one of them is spread. The book's identification lever is tie directionality: friends you name influence you, people who merely name you do not.
- The network has a life of its own. Emergent properties (waves of quitting, clusters of happiness) belong to the graph, not to any individual in it.

## Frameworks and models

**Three Degrees of Influence Rule.** Measured decay per hop in Framingham. Obesity: a friend becoming obese raises your risk about 57 percent at one degree, about 20 percent at two, about 10 percent at three, indistinguishable from zero at four. Happiness: roughly 15 percent at one degree, 10 percent at two, 6 percent at three. Treat three hops as the default influence horizon for any seeded behavior.

**Tie type and strength gradients.** Influence is not uniform across ties. Obesity: mutual friends (both name each other) carry about a 171 percent increase, ego-named friends about 57 percent, alter-named-only near zero; spouses about 37 percent, siblings about 40 percent. Smoking cessation: a spouse quitting cuts your smoking odds by about 67 percent, a friend by about 36 percent, a sibling by about 25 percent. Geography matters less than relationship for behavior, more for emotion: happiness effects require proximity, roughly within a mile.

**Contagion vs homophily vs confounding.** Before calling anything contagious, run all three explanations. The book's tests: longitudinal ordering (whose change came first), directionality asymmetry, and geographic controls. Post-publication critiques (Shalizi and Thomas 2011, Lyons 2011) showed homophily and contagion are generically confounded in observational network data, so treat the magnitudes as directional evidence, not precise parameters.

**Connection and contagion as separate layers.** Connection is topology (transitivity, degree, centrality). Contagion is what flows over it. The same graph carries germs, money, emotions, and norms differently. Pair this with complex contagion (Centola and Macy): Connected's contagions are mostly simple or emotional; behaviors that are costly or deviant need multiple reinforcing exposures and spread poorly over long weak ties.

## Decision rules

- If a behavior clusters in a scene, then test homophily and shared environment before writing "spreading." Ask: whose change came first, and do one-way ties show the effect too? If yes to the latter, suspect homophily.
- If seeding a norm or product, then choose seeds by centrality and mutual ties, not audience size. A mutual tie carries roughly three times the influence of a one-way tie.
- If forecasting reach, then cap organic spread at about three degrees per seed. Sustained diffusion needs re-seeding, not momentum.
- If a group drifts to the periphery of a network (fewer ties, edge positions), then read it as a leading indicator: stigma, withdrawal, or loneliness dynamics are already underway.
- If the behavior is costly, risky, or identity-threatening, then downgrade Connected's simple-contagion expectations and apply complex-contagion logic instead.

## Key terms

- **Three degrees of influence:** empirical rule that social influence decays to noise beyond friends of friends of friends.
- **Hyperdyadic spread:** influence that travels beyond the directly connected pair.
- **Homophily:** the tendency to bond with similar others; mimics contagion in cross-sectional data.
- **Induction:** true person-to-person transmission of a behavior or state.
- **Confounding (shared environment):** clustering caused by common exposure, not ties.
- **Transitivity:** the probability that two of your contacts are connected to each other.
- **Centrality:** how embedded a node is; governs how early it catches what flows.
- **Emotional contagion:** transmission of affect through mimicry and synchronized expression.
