# Diffusion of Innovations (Rogers, 2003)

## The claim

Diffusion is a social process, not a marketing outcome: an innovation spreads through communication channels, over time, among the members of a social system, and most of the variance in how fast it spreads is set by how people perceive the thing, not by its objective merits. Adoption always traces an S-curve, and adopters sort into five categories with distinct sociologies, so you can locate any spreading behavior on the curve and predict its next audience.

## Core claims

- Diffusion has four elements: the innovation, communication channels, time, and the social system. Change any one and the curve changes.
- Cumulative adoption follows an S-curve: slow start among a few, take-off once interpersonal networks activate (roughly 10 to 20 percent adoption), saturation at the ceiling.
- Adopters are normally distributed over time of adoption and partition into innovators (2.5%), early adopters (13.5%), early majority (34%), late majority (34%), and laggards (16%), cut at the mean and standard deviations of adoption time.
- Five perceived attributes explain 49 to 87 percent of the variance in rate of adoption: relative advantage, compatibility, complexity (negative), trialability, and observability.
- Mass media channels create awareness; interpersonal channels drive persuasion and decision. Near-peers matter more than experts or ads at the decision stage.
- Diffusion runs fastest between people who are homophilous, but the new information that starts a diffusion usually crosses a heterophilous link. This is the core tension of every spread.
- Adopters reinvent: an innovation changes meaning and form as it moves down the curve, and reinvention usually speeds adoption.

## Frameworks and models

**Adopter categories (ch. 7).** Time-of-adoption is treated as normally distributed. Cuts: innovators earlier than mean minus 2 sd, early adopters to minus 1 sd, early majority to the mean, late majority to plus 1 sd, laggards beyond. Sociology matters more than the percentages: innovators are cosmopolite risk-tolerators with little local credibility; early adopters are the local opinion leaders and the highest-leverage segment; the late majority adopts from peer pressure and necessity; laggards use the past as reference.

**Five attributes of innovations (ch. 6).** Rate of adoption is a function of perceived relative advantage (better than what it replaces), compatibility (fits existing values and practices), complexity (inverse), trialability (can be tested small), and observability (results are visible to others). Score all five; a hyped thing that fails compatibility and observability will stall.

**Innovation-decision process.** Five stages: knowledge, persuasion, decision, implementation, confirmation. Media dominate knowledge, peers dominate persuasion. Rejection and discontinuance are real outcomes at every stage.

**Diffusion networks and critical mass.** Opinion leaders carry the curve past take-off. Interactive innovations (anything whose value grows with users) have a critical-mass point after which diffusion self-sustains; before it, the same innovation can die. Rogers links this to Granovetter-style individual thresholds.

**Bass model as the quantitative companion.** dN/dt = (p + qN/m)(m minus N), with m the ceiling, p the coefficient of innovation (external, media-driven), q the coefficient of imitation (internal, peer-driven). Typical fits: p around 0.01 to 0.03, q around 0.3 to 0.5; peak adoption at t* = ln(q/p) / (p + q). Rogers gives the sociology, Bass gives the fit. See the bass-new-product-growth chapter for full mechanics.

## Decision rules

- If only innovators carry a signal (niche forums, no local credibility), then do not forecast take-off. If early adopters and opinion leaders pick it up, then forecast the curve.
- If a behavior spreads on media coverage with no peer conversation, then treat it as high p, low q: fast rise, fragile, likely to decay.
- If cumulative adoption crosses roughly 10 to 20 percent of its relevant population, then treat further growth as self-sustaining and shift the question to ceiling and consequences.
- If a trend scores low on compatibility or observability, then discount the hype regardless of relative advantage.
- If the late majority is adopting, then expect reinvention: the meaning has already shifted from what the innovators adopted.
- If an interactive behavior stalls below critical mass, then predict abandonment, not slow growth.

## Key terms

- **Diffusion**: the process by which an innovation is communicated through certain channels over time among members of a social system.
- **Relative advantage**: perceived improvement over the thing it replaces.
- **Compatibility**: fit with existing values, experiences, and needs.
- **Trialability**: how easily the innovation can be tested at small scale.
- **Observability**: how visible the results of adoption are to others.
- **Opinion leader**: a member whose informal influence moves others' adoption; concentrated among early adopters.
- **Homophily / heterophily**: similarity or difference between communicating pairs; diffusion flows easily through homophily but new ideas enter through heterophilous links.
- **Critical mass**: adoption level at which an interactive innovation becomes self-sustaining.
- **Reinvention**: change in the innovation by adopters during implementation.
- **Change agent**: an outsider who promotes adoption on behalf of an institution; succeeds through opinion leaders, not around them.
