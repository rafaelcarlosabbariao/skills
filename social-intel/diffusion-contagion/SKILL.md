# Diffusion and Contagion: How Things Spread

This shelf holds eight works that together form one working theory of spread. They cover the aggregate curve (Bass, Rogers), the graph it runs on (Granovetter, Watts and Strogatz), the physics of what is spreading (Centola and Macy), who deserves credit (Watts and Dodds, Gladwell), and how to prove spread happened at all (Christakis and Fowler). For cultural analysis this is the mechanism layer under every trend call, every audience simulation, and every cascade forecast.

## The unified mental model

Think of any spreading thing as a five-layer stack. Each layer has an owner on this shelf, and a full read touches all five.

**Layer 1: the curve.** Cumulative adoption traces an S-curve when social influence dominates. Bass gives the math: hazard rate p + qF(t), where p is external push (media, advertising, the algorithm) and q is social pull (imitation, word of mouth). If q > p you get the S with an inflection and a forecastable peak at t* = ln(q/p) / (p + q), reached just under half of the potential pool. If q <= p there is no inflection: adoption starts at maximum and decays, which is the signature of a pushed thing, not a chosen one. Rogers gives the same curve its sociology: take-off happens around 10 to 20 percent adoption, when interpersonal networks activate.

**Layer 2: the people on the curve.** Rogers partitions adopters into innovators (2.5%), early adopters (13.5%), early majority (34%), late majority (34%), and laggards (16%), each with a distinct sociology. Innovators are cosmopolite risk-takers with little local credibility. Early adopters are the local opinion leaders and the highest-leverage segment. The rate of adoption itself is mostly set by five perceived attributes: relative advantage, compatibility, complexity (negative), trialability, and observability, which explain 49 to 87 percent of the variance. At the individual level, the microfoundation is a threshold: each person activates when enough of their neighbors have.

**Layer 3: the graph.** Granovetter shows that novel information enters through weak ties, because strong ties are transitive and therefore redundant: your close friends already know what you know. All bridges between clusters are weak ties. Watts and Strogatz formalize the topology: real social graphs are small worlds, highly clustered yet a few hops across, because a tiny number of random shortcuts collapses path length while clustering stays intact. Locality is not insulation. A scene that feels sealed is two shortcuts from anything.

**Layer 4: what is spreading.** Centola and Macy supply the master fork of the whole shelf. Simple contagions (information, news, a meme, a virus) transmit on one exposure and ride shortcuts and weak ties at small-world speed. Complex contagions (costly, risky, or identity-laden behavior) need reinforcement from multiple sources, so a threshold T >= 2 applies, and the finding inverts: long random ties arrive alone at the far end, below threshold, so they slow or kill behavioral spread. What carries behavior across communities is a wide bridge, multiple overlapping ties between the same two clusters, at least as wide as the threshold. Awareness and behavior obey different physics.

**Layer 5: credit and proof.** Watts and Dodds show in simulation that large cascades are driven by a critical mass of easily influenced people influencing each other, not by rare special seeds. Whether any cascade is possible depends on the system sitting inside a cascade window in connectivity-threshold space; outside it, no seed works, however famous. Gladwell's Tipping Point supplies the lever inventory (messenger, message, context) but its Law of the Few must be carried with the Watts correction attached. Christakis and Fowler add the evidentiary discipline: clustering has three rival explanations, contagion, homophily, and shared environment, and only one of them is spread. Influence that is real decays to noise past three degrees of separation.

## Through-lines

1. **Everything S-curves; the question is the mechanism.** Bass, Rogers, and the threshold models all produce the same shape from different engines. The analytical work is decomposing the shape: is the engine external push (p), peer imitation (q), or threshold percolation? The shape alone never tells you.
2. **Simple versus complex is the first question, always.** Every model on this shelf silently assumes one or the other. Weak-tie speed, small-world speed, and shortcut logic hold for simple contagions. Wide bridges, local redundancy, and concentrated seeding hold for complex ones. Classify before you model; the cost of the behavior (money, effort, identity risk, social sanction) sets the threshold.
3. **Bridges carry all cross-cluster traffic, and width beats length for behavior.** Granovetter's weak ties, Watts-Strogatz shortcuts, and Centola-Macy wide bridges are three resolutions of the same object. A single thin bridge moves the news. Crossing a costly practice needs a bridge wide enough to deliver T exposures.
4. **The system beats the seed.** Watts and Dodds, the cascade window, and Bass's q all point the same way: whether the forest is dry matters more than who lights the match. Cascade outcomes are intrinsically high variance; identical systems seeded identically produce a dud or a wildfire. Post-hoc influencer credit is mostly narrative.
5. **Spread must be proven, not assumed.** Connected's three-explanation test (contagion, homophily, shared environment) comes before any "X is spreading" claim. Whose change came first, and do one-way ties show the effect too? If yes to the latter, suspect homophily.
6. **Pools are finite.** Bass's m means decline after a peak is arithmetic, not death. Check pool exhaustion before writing an obituary.

## Where the works disagree

**The few versus the forest.** Gladwell says rare Connectors, Mavens, and Salesmen do the transmission work. Watts and Dodds formalize the claim and break it: influentials trigger big cascades only modestly more often than average people, their per-link advantage is roughly nil, and the parameters that would make targeting them pay off are unobservable in advance. Rogers holds a defensible middle position: opinion leaders (concentrated among early adopters) matter at the persuasion stage of a specific individual decision, which is a narrower claim than being the engine of population-scale cascades. Use Gladwell for the lever inventory, Watts-Dodds for seeding strategy, Rogers for the stage model.

**The strength versus the weakness of long ties.** Granovetter and Watts-Strogatz establish that long weak ties and shortcuts accelerate spread. Centola and Macy show the result holds only for simple contagions and inverts for complex ones: rewiring destroys the local redundancy that delivers the second exposure. Both are right about different cargo. The 1983 boundary condition also applies: only bridging weak ties matter; a weak tie inside your own cluster buys nothing.

**How solid is the contagion evidence.** Connected's Framingham magnitudes (obesity, smoking cessation, happiness, loneliness spreading to three degrees) are directionally powerful, but the chapter carries the post-publication critique: Shalizi and Thomas (2011) and Lyons (2011) showed homophily and contagion are generically confounded in observational network data. Treat the magnitudes as directional evidence and starting parameters, never as precise estimates.

**Aggregate versus structural.** Bass fits and forecasts from sales data alone with no graph, which is its power and its blindness: p and q are the aggregate shadow of thresholds and topology it cannot see. When the question is "when does this peak," Bass wins. When the question is "will it cross into that community," Bass has nothing to say and the network works take over.

## Which tool when

Reach for **Bass** when you have a time series and need a lifecycle position or a peak forecast. Reach for **Rogers** when you need to know who is carrying a trend and who adopts next, or to score why adoption is fast or slow. Reach for **Granovetter** and **Watts-Strogatz** when the question is where novelty enters a scene and how fast information crosses. Reach for **Centola-Macy** the moment the spreading thing costs something to adopt. Reach for **Watts-Dodds** whenever anyone says the word influencer. Reach for **Connected** before asserting that anything is spreading at all, and for per-hop decay parameters. Reach for **Gladwell** for the message and context levers, never for targeting.
