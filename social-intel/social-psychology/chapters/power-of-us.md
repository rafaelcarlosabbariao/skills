# The Power of Us

## The claim

Shared identity is an operating system, not an accessory. When a group identity becomes salient it rewrites what people perceive, believe, value, and do, and it can be switched on by almost nothing. Leaders, brands, and movements that understand this act as identity entrepreneurs: they do not find audiences, they build a "we."

## Core claims

- Group identity forms from minimal cues. Tajfel's minimal group paradigm showed that arbitrary assignment (a coin flip, a claimed preference for Klee vs Kandinsky) produces in-group favoritism, including sacrificing absolute gains to maximize relative advantage over the out-group.
- Identity filters perception before reasoning starts. Partisans literally see different games (Hastorf and Cantril's Dartmouth vs Princeton study); Van Bavel's fMRI work shows minimal team membership can override race in early face processing.
- Belief serves belonging. Accuracy is one goal among several, and protecting group identity or signaling loyalty routinely outweighs it. Much "misinformation belief" is expressive cheering, not conviction.
- Most intergroup conflict is driven by in-group love, not out-group hate. Favoritism toward "us" does the bulk of the discriminatory work (Brewer), which changes what interventions can succeed.
- Identities are fluid and plural. Which identity governs behavior depends on salience in context; leaders and situations, not fixed traits, decide which "we" is active.
- Dissent is an identity behavior. Packer's normative conflict model: the most committed members are the ones who challenge harmful group norms, so loyal internal criticism signals investment, not exit.
- Moral-emotional language is identity fuel, and its spread stays largely inside the ideological in-group network (see moral contagion below).

## Frameworks and models

**Minimal group paradigm** (Tajfel 1970). Random assignment to meaningless categories; allocation matrices then reveal maximum differentiation: subjects give up in-group profit to widen the gap over the out-group. Practical mechanic: any visible sorting cue can seed full group dynamics.

**Social identity and self-categorization**. The self is a stack of identities; the active one is set by salience, a function of fit (does the category explain the situation) and accessibility (how chronically primed it is). Behavior follows the norms of whichever identity is on.

**Identity-based model of belief** (Van Bavel and Pereira 2018). Belief formation weighs accuracy goals against belonging, status, and moral goals. Predicts expressive responding (Schaffner and Luks's inauguration-photo study) and predicts that raising accuracy stakes, not adding facts, moves stated beliefs. Parameter worth carrying: small accuracy incentives shrink partisan factual gaps by roughly half (Bullock et al. 2015).

**Identity entrepreneurship** (via Reicher and Haslam). Effective leaders define the category ("who counts as us"), embody its prototype, set its norms, and name its rival. Influence flows from being seen as "one of us," not from position.

**Normative conflict model of dissent** (Packer 2008). Weakly identified members conform or leave; strongly identified members dissent when norms harm the group. Dissent rate among committed members is a leading indicator of norm change.

**Moral contagion** (Brady et al. 2017, PNAS). Diffusion multiplier of ~1.2 per moral-emotional word; spread is in-network bounded. Gives a countable lexical signal for identity mobilization.

## Decision rules

- If a claim spreads fast but stays inside one identity network, read it as loyalty signaling, not information transfer; forecast containment at the network boundary.
- If a new "we" label appears alongside a named enemy, log identity entrepreneurship in progress; track the entrepreneur and the prototype they perform, not just the content.
- If minimal cues (an emoji, a slang term, a badge) begin sorting people, expect full in-group favoritism dynamics quickly; do not wait for substantive disagreement to appear.
- If you need what an audience actually believes, add cost or accuracy stakes; stated belief under high identity salience is expressive by default.
- If committed insiders start publicly criticizing their group, read norm shift, not collapse.
- If two groups escalate, diagnose in-group love vs out-group hate first; favoritism-driven conflict responds to superordinate framing, hate-driven conflict does not.

## Key terms

- **Minimal group paradigm**: arbitrary category assignment that still produces in-group favoritism.
- **Salience**: the contextual switch that decides which identity is currently running behavior.
- **Identity entrepreneur**: an actor who defines, embodies, and mobilizes a group identity.
- **Identity-based belief**: belief formation in which belonging goals compete with, and often beat, accuracy goals.
- **Expressive responding**: stating a false belief as a loyalty signal rather than a conviction.
- **In-group favoritism**: preference for us that drives discrimination without requiring hatred of them.
- **Normative dissent**: criticism from committed members aimed at correcting harmful group norms.
- **Moral contagion**: increased diffusion of messages carrying moral-emotional language, bounded within identity networks.
- **Superordinate identity**: a broader shared category that recasts rivals as one "we."
