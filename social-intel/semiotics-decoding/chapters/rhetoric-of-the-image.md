# Rhetoric of the Image

## The claim

Every image carries three messages at once: a linguistic message, a coded (connoted) iconic message, and a non-coded (denoted) iconic message. The denoted layer, the photograph's apparent record of what was simply there, is not innocent: it naturalizes the connoted layer, making a constructed cultural message feel like plain fact. Barthes proves this by decomposing one Panzani pasta advertisement, still the most-taught worked example of semiotic decomposition.

## Core claims

- Advertising is the ideal decode material because its signification is intentional and emphatic: signs are built a priori for optimal reading, so nothing in the frame is accidental.
- The linguistic message has two possible functions relative to the image: anchorage, which fixes the image's floating chain of meanings on one preferred reading, and relay, where text and image advance the meaning together.
- The connoted message is assembled from discontinuous signs. In the Panzani ad: the half-open bag and market produce signify "return from the market" (freshness, domestic care); the tricolored hues (yellow, green, red) plus the name Panzani, by its assonance, signify "Italianicity"; the composition quotes the still life and signifies aesthetic culture.
- The photograph is a "message without a code" at the denoted level: an analogical record whose temporality is "having-been-there." This is exactly what lets it launder connotation as nature.
- Reading connotation requires lexicons: bodies of cultural knowledge (practical, national, cultural, aesthetic) the viewer must already hold. Different viewers with different lexicons extract different sets of signifieds from the same image.
- The full set of connotators in a culture's images forms a rhetoric; the set of connoted signifieds forms an ideology. Decoding images is therefore reading ideology, not describing pictures.

## Frameworks and models

**The three-message model.** For any image, separate: (1) linguistic message, everything written in or around the image; (2) denoted iconic message, the literal inventory of objects and scene; (3) connoted iconic message, the cultural meanings the arrangement triggers. Order matters: transcribe denotation first, then list connotators one by one as signifier-signified pairs, then classify the text's function. No math; the operative quantities are the count of connotators found and the count of lexicons a viewer needs to receive them.

**Anchorage vs relay.** Anchorage is repressive: it selects among the image's polysemy ("this is what you should see"). Relay is additive: text carries meaning the image cannot (dialogue, sequence). Press and advertising are anchorage-heavy; comics and film are relay-heavy.

**Naturalization.** Connotation rides on denotation. Because the photo seems to merely record, the myth it carries arrives as fact. The mechanism from Mythologies, myth as connotation masquerading as nature, gets its operating diagram here.

## Decision rules

- If reading any cultural image, then inventory the denoted layer first, in flat literal language, before naming a single connotation. Skipping this step is how analysts hallucinate meanings.
- If a caption or on-image text is present, then classify it as anchorage or relay before interpreting the image; anchorage tells you which reading the producer is paying to enforce.
- If a sign reads as obvious or natural, then treat that as the finding: name the connotator doing the work and the lexicon it assumes. "Natural" is the signature of successful myth.
- If two audience segments read the same artifact differently, then diff their lexicons, not their attitudes. The disagreement is usually upstream, in what codes each side possesses.
- If decoding AI-generated imagery, then note that "having-been-there" is void: the denoted layer no longer certifies anything, so naturalization must be smuggled in through style instead. Watch for connotation without a real denotation under it.
- If building a message, then choose anchorage deliberately; an unanchored image will be read through whichever lexicon the loudest audience holds.

## Key terms

- **Denotation:** the literal, first-order message; the objects actually in the frame.
- **Connotation:** the second-order cultural meaning triggered by how the denoted material is arranged.
- **Connotator:** a signifier of connotation; one discontinuous sign within the image (the tricolor palette, the string bag).
- **Anchorage:** text that fixes the image's polysemy and directs the reader to one meaning.
- **Relay:** text that complements the image and carries meaning the image cannot.
- **Polysemy:** an image's inherent openness to multiple readings, resolved by anchorage or by the viewer's lexicons.
- **Lexicon:** a body of cultural knowledge a viewer needs to receive a given connotation.
- **Message without a code:** the photograph's denoted layer, an analogical record rather than a coded sign system.
- **Italianicity:** Barthes's worked example of a connoted signified, the condensed essence of "Italian" available to a non-Italian audience.
