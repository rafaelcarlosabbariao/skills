# Patterns: semiotics-decoding shelf

Reusable recipes that emerge across the five works. Each gives the name, when to use it, the steps, and the source works. These are the shelf in executable form; the theory behind each step lives in the chapters.

## 1. Semiotic pre-pass

**When to use.** The daily default. Run before naming any mechanism in a trend read, on any artifact in any of the eight domains.

**Steps.**
1. Decode the sign: state what the artifact literally is and what the culture maps it to (the cultural unit, not the referent).
2. Name the code: which convention makes this meaning available, and which community holds that convention.
3. State the gap: what the sign promises versus what the system behind it delivers.

**Source works.** A Theory of Semiotics (code, cultural unit); Mythologies (the pre-pass rule is stated in its decision rules).

## 2. Three-message image pass

**When to use.** Any visual artifact: an ad, a viral outfit, a menu aesthetic, a physique post, a product render, an AI image.

**Steps.**
1. Transcribe the denoted layer first, in flat literal language: the inventory of objects and scene. Do not name a single connotation yet; skipping this step is how analysts hallucinate meanings.
2. List connotators one by one as signifier-signified pairs (e.g. the tricolored yellow-green-red hues signify Italianicity).
3. For each connotator, name the lexicon a viewer needs to receive it (practical, national, cultural, aesthetic, platform-native).
4. Classify all text in or around the image as anchorage (fixes one reading) or relay (adds meaning the image cannot carry). Anchorage tells you which reading the producer is paying to enforce.
5. For generative imagery, check the having-been-there: if the denoted layer certifies nothing, note where naturalization is being smuggled in through style instead.

**Source works.** Rhetoric of the Image (the whole procedure); A Theory of Semiotics (iconism as convention, for step 5).

## 3. Myth check with the figures checklist

**When to use.** Any cultural claim that presents as obvious, natural, or timeless; brand confession; both-sides talk; proverb-voiced takes.

**Steps.**
1. Treat the claim as a candidate myth: ask what history was removed and who benefits from the removal.
2. Scan for the recurring figures: inoculation (a small confessed flaw shielding the main one), privation of history (the object appears from nowhere), identification (the other reduced to a version of the self or exoticized), tautology ("it is what it is"), neither-norism (rejecting both poles to avoid the question), quantification of quality (effects counted, meaning skipped), statement of fact (proverb-voiced ideology).
3. Log the figure rather than arguing the content; a position that refuses paraphrase or refuses both poles is data.
4. To date the myth, find the moment its vocabulary stopped needing justification. Naturalization has a timestamp.

**Source works.** Mythologies.

## 4. Counter-myth construction

**When to use.** When a read or a client deliverable needs to counter a naturalized frame, not just describe it.

**Steps.**
1. Do not fact-check the first order. Myth concedes the facts and captures the frame; refutation by facts alone fails.
2. Surface the second order: show the form, the concept attached to it, and the moment of draining.
3. Alternatively, build an artificial myth that exposes the form, i.e. mythify the myth so the second layer becomes visible.

**Source works.** Mythologies.

## 5. Reading-position distribution audit

**When to use.** Any message that lands differently across segments: a divided trend story, contested platform announcements, audience data that looks inconsistent.

**Steps.**
1. Identify the preferred reading first: the meaning the text is built to invite, and the institutional backing holding it in place.
2. Classify each observed segment's reading as dominant-hegemonic, negotiated, or oppositional. Test for opposition before diagnosing misunderstanding; audiences that understand a frame perfectly and reject it are the norm in contested culture.
3. Log negotiated codes explicitly: a segment endorsing the frame in the abstract while defecting in its own situation is the modal position, not a contradiction.
4. Report the distribution of positions per segment as the finding. Never adjudicate the correct reading.
5. Track the distribution over time. The modal position migrating (dominant to negotiated to oppositional) is a leading indicator of a shift, ahead of any change in the messages themselves.

**Source works.** Encoding/Decoding (typology, Morley's empirical test); A Theory of Semiotics (split readings as data about code distribution).

## 6. Lexicon diff

**When to use.** Two audience segments read the same artifact differently and you need to explain why, not just report it.

**Steps.**
1. List the connotations each segment extracts from the artifact.
2. For each divergent connotation, name the lexicon it requires.
3. Diff the lexicons, not the attitudes. The disagreement is usually upstream, in which codes each side possesses, not in what each side feels.

**Source works.** Rhetoric of the Image; Encoding/Decoding (frameworks of knowledge on the decoding side).

## 7. Paradigm reconstruction

**When to use.** Reading what a choice signals: a word, a garment, a plating style, an interface pattern. Also when encoding audience agents.

**Steps.**
1. Identify the slot the choice occupies (the syntagmatic position).
2. Reconstruct the paradigm: the live alternatives that could have filled that slot for this community at this moment.
3. Read the choice against what it excluded. The signal is the difference, not the item.
4. For language reads, track paradigm substitutions (which word replaced which in the same slot) rather than just counting new coinages.

**Source works.** Course in General Linguistics.

## 8. Value-drift neighbor check

**When to use.** A term or sign whose meaning is shifting.

**Steps.**
1. Read the current state synchronically first; origin stories do not explain what the sign does today for the people using it.
2. Check the neighbors: value is relational, so a drift in one term implies repricing across the set. Map which adjacent terms moved.
3. Expect stability against any single actor and drift over time. Nobody can move the sign alone; everybody moves it together.

**Source works.** Course in General Linguistics.

## 9. Naturalized-code audit

**When to use.** A meaning treated as obvious by everyone in a scene; a dispute presenting as denotative ("that is just what it says").

**Steps.**
1. Treat the obviousness itself as the finding. Name the code that makes the meaning obvious.
2. Name the community holding the convention.
3. Ask who encoded it and what infrastructure keeps it in place (institutional power, professional codes, common sense).
4. When a fight presents as being about literal meaning, look for the naturalized code doing the work at connotation.

**Source works.** Encoding/Decoding; Mythologies; Course in General Linguistics; A Theory of Semiotics (the convergent "natural is the tell" rule).

## 10. Slop sort

**When to use.** Judging whether content is commodity replication or genuine invention; any human-versus-AI-slop reading.

**Steps.**
1. Sort by ratio: ratio facilis output (tokens stamped from an existing expression type) is commodity; ratio difficilis output (expression invented directly from content, no type existing) carries the cultural signal.
2. Run the langue test: generative output reproduces syntagms fluently but has no community holding its langue. Look for value errors, near-miss word choices against the live paradigm.
3. For images, run the having-been-there check from pattern 2: connotation with no real denotation under it is the slop signature.
4. Track the label itself: calling content "slop" is an oppositional decoding of material encoded as sincere, and the label spreading is a code shift worth logging. Track "human-made" as an emerging counter-myth, and track the first-order content and its second-order signified (effortlessness, abundance) separately.

**Source works.** A Theory of Semiotics (ratio, lie criterion); Course in General Linguistics (langue test); Rhetoric of the Image (having-been-there); Encoding/Decoding (the label as oppositional decoding); Mythologies (counter-myth).

## 11. Audience rule-set build (langue from parole)

**When to use.** Building or refreshing an audience rule set and its simulated agents.

**Steps.**
1. Treat the corpus of posts as parole. Do not report parole as insight; infer the langue, the shared rules that make those posts sayable.
2. Compile the rule set as a code inventory (which codes and subcodes the audience runs) and a lexicon inventory (which bodies of cultural knowledge it holds: national, subcultural, aesthetic, platform-native).
3. Encode agents with paradigmatic choice sets, not word lists, so the sim models what members would substitute, not just what they said.
4. Give agents different subcodes and different decoders. Never give all agents the same decoder; encode the message once, decode it per agent, with dominant/negotiated/oppositional weights per segment.
5. Where reading position is measurable, also score followers by Barthes's positions per sign (producer, mythologist, consumer) and simulate how a creative choice signifies at the second order, not just what it shows.

**Source works.** Course in General Linguistics (langue/parole, paradigms); Encoding/Decoding (per-agent decoders, position weights); Rhetoric of the Image (lexicon inventory); A Theory of Semiotics (code inventory, divergent decoding in the sim); Mythologies (position scoring).

## 12. Undercoding watch (naming race)

**When to use.** A behavior or scene that has no stable name yet; competing labels in circulation.

**Steps.**
1. Treat the situation as undercoding in progress: rough provisional codes being assigned to not-yet-understood material.
2. Track the competing provisional labels as candidates, not noise.
3. When one label wins, read it: the winning label reveals which code won.

**Source works.** A Theory of Semiotics.

## 13. Overcoding fragmentation read

**When to use.** A field where microgenres, taxonomies, or aesthetic labels are proliferating fast; any taste-versus-algorithm reading.

**Steps.**
1. Recognize fast overcoding as a fragmentation signal: expect audience splits along subcode boundaries and read for those boundaries.
2. Treat recommenders as industrial overcoding machines that narrow subcodes per user, and as anchorage at scale, fixing which reading of an artifact wins and starving oppositional ones.
3. Log re-anchoring events: a community re-reading an artifact against the platform's caption is taste asserting itself.
4. Read "the algorithm knows me" as a candidate myth: a recommender's output naturalized into personal essence.

**Source works.** A Theory of Semiotics (overcoding, subcode boundaries); Rhetoric of the Image (anchorage at scale); Encoding/Decoding (preferred readings optimized, oppositional starved); Mythologies (naturalization).

## 14. Frame-first forecasting

**When to use.** Any forecast run touching cultural signs; durability estimates for trends.

**Steps.**
1. Forecast re-codings, not artifacts. A sign-function decoupling from its content is the leading indicator of a shift.
2. Score naturalization depth as a durability variable: a trend still arguing for itself decays faster than one that has become "just how things are."
3. Track connotator inventories: which signifiers currently carry "authentic," "healthy," or "masculine" drift before the values themselves do.
4. Watch the modal decoding position per segment; migration precedes message change.
5. Forecast the convention-holding community's cohesion, not the sign itself. Arbitrariness means no natural floor under any sign; when the community fragments, the sign can flip fast.

**Source works.** A Theory of Semiotics (re-codings); Mythologies (naturalization depth); Rhetoric of the Image (connotator drift); Encoding/Decoding (position migration); Course in General Linguistics (community cohesion).
