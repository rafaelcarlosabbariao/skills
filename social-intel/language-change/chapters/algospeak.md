# Algospeak

## The claim

Algorithmic recommendation is now the selection environment for language. The narrow sense of "algospeak" — euphemisms invented to dodge content moderation, "unalive" for "kill" — is a special case of something much larger: ranked feeds now decide who is exposed to which words, how those words travel, how popular they get, and how fast they die. Language, memes and metadata have become the same object. A word is a hashtag is a demographic.

Two corrections carry the book. The first is that none of the underlying human processes are new. Euphemising death, evading censors, inventing in-group slang, appropriating the vocabulary of subcultures perceived as cool — all of it has a long documented history, and the algorithmic era is the same process in a faster medium. The second is that "do algorithms change how we talk" is the wrong question. Human-algorithm interaction is a complex system, and the linguistic outcomes are emergent: they exist only in the loop, and cannot be assigned to either side.

## Core claims

- Algospeak is a **sociolect**, not a substitution list. It is code-switched by domain of use, and it is near-identical across TikTok, Reels and Shorts because successful creators cross-post and therefore write to the strictest constraint in the set.
- **Content moderation drives linguistic creativity.** Ban a word and a replacement appears, because the underlying idea and the desire to discuss it persist. Better moderation produces more words, not fewer.
- **Word survival is decided by four factors, none of which is quality**: frequency, unobtrusiveness, endurance factor (the count of independent contexts it works in), and whether it fills a semantic gap.
- **Obtrusiveness kills.** A word that announces itself as slang dies when the last adopters arrive, because its value to the innovators was exclusivity. Aleksic's field test: if someone can make fun of you for saying it, it is obtrusive.
- The specific mechanism is **engagement optimization**, not "algorithms" in general. Goodhart's law applies: engagement became a target and stopped being a good metric, so the feed rewards ragebait and misinformation as readily as anything else.
- **Filter bubbles are word factories.** Niche communities have the pressing need to name things, so they are the innovators in every case the book traces. Context collapse is how those words escape, and escape is what poisons them.
- Once a word crosses the bubble boundary, **the originating community loses the ability to police it**. Offline, misuse gets corrected socially; online there is no correction channel and the boundary is a gradient rather than a line.
- **Poe's law is a transmission mechanism**, not a footnote. Sincere content read as a joke earns likes; ironic content read as sincere earns outrage. Both are engagement, so edgy material travels either way.
- The **Matthew effect** governs reach: content marginally better at capturing attention performs exponentially better, because a share deficit compounds through every subsequent round.
- Words no longer diffuse only human to human. They now move **human → algorithm → human**, and the algorithmic leg is what makes the modern cycle both faster and harder to trace.
- Attention is the scarce resource and the accent is a retention device. The "influencer accent" is not affectation; it is uptalk, emphatic prosody and vowel lengthening doing measurable work against the scroll.

## Frameworks and models

**Linguistic Whac-A-Mole.** Ban → substitute → ban → substitute, indefinitely. The historical series is leetspeak (`5U1C1D3`), grawlixes and bowdlerisation in newspaper comics from 1901, minced oaths ("heck", "gosh", Mailer's "fug"), Cockney rhyming slang, and the Jesus fish. The game is formally unwinnable: a 2015 Georgia Tech study found that flagging every homophone substitution in Chinese would false-positive roughly 20 percent of all posts, rendering the platform unusable.

**The four survival factors.** Aleksic's operative model, derived from Rogers' *innovation-decision process*, which splits adoption into two decisions — start using it, then continue or abandon it.
1. **Frequency.** Is it culturally trending. A word can keep trending because it was trending; the feedback loop is the point.
2. **Unobtrusiveness.** Does it feel natural or forced. "Cancel" entered unnoticed and survived; "yeet" and "on fleek" were marked as Gen Z slang and did not.
3. **Endurance factor.** How many independent phrasal templates and grammatical situations it appears in. One template means it dies with the meme.
4. **Semantic gap.** Is there a concept with no existing word, or a funnier/better way to say something. Without a gap there is nothing to hold the word in place once the joke is over.

**The chair emoji as control case.** September 2021, a coordinated prank makes 🪑 the replacement laughing emoji; searches spike; by November it is gone. Maximum frequency, zero unobtrusiveness (the joke *was* that it was unnatural), zero endurance, no semantic gap. Contrast 💀, adopted at the same time and retained, because it kept a live function: differentiating younger users from the 😂 generation.

**The euphemism treadmill (Pinker), algorithmically accelerated.** Historically, words for offensive things went bad through social pejoration and had to be replaced. The platform now performs that role directly: it labels a word undesirable, which moves the treadmill faster without suppressing the topic. `le$bian` → `wlw`; `suicide` → `unalive` → `un@l!ve` → `sewerslide` / `ropemaxx`.

**The engagement treadmill.** Certain content earns engagement → more creators make it → more people engage → more creators make it. This is why a keyword trends because the algorithm decided it would, and why "rizz" content existed before "rizz" was widely known.

**Emergent human-algorithm effects (Narayanan).** Refuse both "algorithms radicalise people" and "algorithms only reflect existing behavior". The system is nonlinear and the results exist only inside it. The book instantiates the same diagram four times: the in-group/echo-chamber loop, the attention-tactic loop, the trend-amplification loop, and the censorship Whac-A-Mole loop.

**Sociolect, domain of use, code-switching, communication accommodation.** Standard sociolinguistic apparatus, applied to the feed. Everyone code-switches; the algospeak sociolect has a domain of use (online) which breaks down for speakers too young to distinguish domains — which is exactly how "unalive" reached middle-school hallways and a museum placard.

**Diffusion of innovations (Rogers) applied to words.** Innovators are always niche communities with a pressing need to name their own experience — ball houses, incels on 4chan, the mental-health community, K-pop and Swiftie fandoms. Everyone is an early adopter of some vocabulary. The laggards' arrival is the toxic dose: at that point the innovators either find a permanent use for the word or drop it.

**Context collapse (Marwick & boyd).** Speech intended for one audience reaches audiences who reprocess it in a different context and reuse it in a new way. Aleksic's cases are "acoustic", "neurospicy" and "touch of the 'tism" escaping the autistic community and returning as insults; the same shape covers ball slang and hood irony.

**Phrasal and meme templates as endurance.** "on X", "she X on my Y till I Z", "it's giving X", "not you doing X", "in my X era", "X ahh". Each template is another independent context, and each reiteration outlives the meme that produced it while keeping the word feeling fresh.

**The linguistic founder effect.** The first users of a platform set its linguistic culture, and later users inherit it as the prestige standard. Kardashian/Valley uptalk → the 2015 "YouTube voice" → the TikTok-era influencer accent. Three variants exist: **lifestyle** (uptalk, vowel lengthening, the most-mocked because it is used by young women), **entertainment** (MrBeast, maximal enthusiasm), and **educational** (fast, high pitch variation).

**Algorithmic optimization replacing SEO.** The "-core" suffix is metadata, not aesthetics. 150+ variants on the Aesthetics Wiki, 6,000+ Spotify microgenres, and TikTok's own 2021 business page saying the quiet part out loud: *"subcultures are the new demographics"* and *"your brand becomes part of their identity."* Sophie of `cutandcropped` used "word", "hashtag" and "demographic" interchangeably in interview, and was right to.

**Long tail (Anderson) → enshittification (Doctorow) → Flanderization.** Microlabels compartmentalise identity so it can be commodified; the platform degrades for users then for businesses then for both; and creators self-caricature toward whatever the feed rewards. Walton's "Categories of Art" is the sting: each label carries rules, so more labels means less freedom, not more.

**The appropriation gradient.** Innovator in-group with a pressing need → adjacent group seeking its prestige → progressively peripheral circles → mainstream. Attribution decays geometrically (if half credit the source, a quarter do at the next round, and the true rate is far below half). Two terminal states, both losses for the origin: discarded as passé ("fleek") or normalized as standard ("cool").

**Poe's law as transmission.** Any sincere expression of extreme views can be read as satire and vice versa. Applied to the incel-to-mainstream pipeline: the joke reading spread the words, the sincere reading manifested the harms, and canthal-tilt eyeliner tutorials and jaw-surgery searches rose after the jokes went viral.

## Decision rules

- If a term is being discussed as "Gen Z slang" or "brainrot", score it **obtrusive** and shorten the half-life estimate. Meta-commentary is the death signal.
- If a term is familiar words in a new sense — "cancel", "function", "side-eye", "opp" — score it **unobtrusive** and lengthen the estimate. Nobody noticed it arriving, so nobody will notice it staying.
- Before forecasting persistence, **count the independent phrasal templates**. One template means it dies with the meme. Three or more means it outlives the meme.
- If a term has **no semantic gap** — an entrenched word already covers the concept — forecast a fad, not an entry. Run the chair-emoji test.
- **Never attribute a slang term to the generation using it.** Ask which in-group had a pressing need to name that thing. Aleksic's rule of thumb: if a word did not come from 4chan, it probably came from African American English.
- If an in-group word is spreading outward, **forecast pejoration, not adoption**. The origin community loses enforcement first, meaning second.
- **Do not read a moderation-driven substitution as a decline in the topic.** Suppression moves the word, not the conversation. A rising substitution rate is evidence the topic is hot.
- If asked whether the platform caused a change, **refuse the framing**. Name the human need, the platform incentive, and the loop, or say you cannot trace it.
- Any engagement metric offered as evidence of what people want is a **revealed** preference. Say which you are reporting; the gap between stated and revealed is widening and is itself the finding.
- If a term arrives with an ironic frame, **forecast for both doses**. Some fraction of the audience is receiving it sincerely, and that fraction is where the harm lands.
- **Discount creators' self-reports about their own speech.** Roughly 30 percent of creators with an identifiable influencer accent say they never tried to acquire one.
- Before calling a term dead, check **who abandoned it**. The origin community leaving and the periphery leaving are different findings with opposite implications.
- When a platform bans a search term, expect the community to persist under a substitute and **budget for the substitute in the harvest**, not for silence.

## Quantities the book actually supplies

Treat all of these as direction and rough magnitude. The surveys are author-run convenience samples of **adults reporting on children's speech**, which measures adult awareness and therefore lags actual usage; the lecture polls are self-selected audiences at three elite institutions.

- **TELUS International, 2022**: ~75% of Americans have encountered algospeak; 30% actively use coded language to circumvent moderation.
- **Aleksic survey, March 2024** (n ≈ 1,700 middle-school parents and teachers): 80% had heard "rizz", 55% "gyat", 60% "no because".
- **Aleksic survey, July 2024** (n ≈ 2,000): >75% had heard "gyat" — up from 55% six months earlier; >50% for each of "yass", "tea", "slay", "queen", "giving", "ate"; >60% for "opp". 91% attribute the learning to social media; 84% are sure the driver is friends' usage; 57% believe the words serve a community-building function; >85% are confident the children do not know the terms are African American English.
- **Incel-vocabulary recognition, lecture polls**: Georgetown, March 2024, ~40%; Stanford, two months later, 80%; Oberlin, four months after that, 100%. Roughly 60 points in six months.
- **Influencer poll** (n = 45, 10k–3M followers): 64% consciously modify their speech online; two-thirds of those cite platform metrics as the reason. The residual ~30% report never having tried.
- **MrBeast speech rate**: ~170 wpm early, just under 200 wpm now, against ~140 wpm for the average English speaker. Standard deviation fell from 42 wpm to 14 wpm — the convergence is the evidence of deliberate tuning.
- **Virality exponent**: a post shared 20% less reaches roughly 90% fewer people, because the deficit compounds through every round.
- **Attribution decay**: geometric. At a 50% crediting rate, 25% at round two; the observed rate is far below 50%.
- **Georgia Tech, 2015**: flagging every Chinese homophone substitution would remove ~20% of all posts as false positives.
- **Retention**: a bad first impression can cost 50% of retention inside the first second.
- **UT Austin**: ~60% decline in the Texas accent, 1980–2013.
- **Label inflation**: 150+ "-core" aesthetics on the Aesthetics Wiki; 6,000+ Spotify microgenres.
- **Language coverage**: ~7,000 world languages; YouTube supports 80, Google 240, Wikipedia 345. Hindi is 7.5% of the world's population and <0.1% of websites; German is ~1% of speakers and 5.5% of the web.

## Key terms

- **Algospeak**: originally, evasive vocabulary invented to avoid algorithmic moderation. Aleksic widens it to the whole sociolect produced by algorithmic selection on language.
- **Sociolect**: the language variety used by a particular social group. The frat house, the family, the fandom, the feed.
- **Domain of use**: the setting a variety belongs to. Algospeak's is online, and it leaks when speakers cannot yet distinguish domains.
- **Obtrusiveness**: how noticeable or forced a word feels. The single strongest death predictor on this shelf.
- **Endurance factor**: the reason a word keeps getting used after the meme that carried it has ended; operationally, its count of independent contexts.
- **Semantic gap**: a concept with no existing word. Words that fill one stay.
- **Euphemism treadmill** (Pinker): the endless replacement cycle for words about offensive things, now driven by moderation rather than only by pejoration.
- **Engagement treadmill** (Aleksic): the self-reinforcing loop between what earns engagement and what creators make.
- **Whac-A-Mole**: the ban-and-substitute cycle between moderators and users.
- **Bowdlerisation**: respelling an offensive word. **Grawlix**: the comic-strip symbol string (`$#*!`). **Minced oath**: a phonetic softening ("heck", "seggs", "fug").
- **Voldemorting** (van der Nagel): deliberately avoiding a keyword so the content does not surface under it.
- **Context collapse** (Marwick & boyd): speech for one audience reaching another that reprocesses it.
- **Filter bubble / echo chamber / cyberbalkanisation**: the personalised feed, the reinforcing environment, and the splintering of the net into interest groups. All three are word factories.
- **Fanilect**: a fandom's sociolect (Swiftie, K-pop). **Familect**: a family's.
- **Linguistic founder effect**: the first users of a platform set its linguistic culture.
- **Prestige dialect**: a variety associated with a desirable social class. The influencer accents are self-taught prestige dialects.
- **Uptalk / emphatic prosody / floor holding**: rising terminal intonation, stressing more words than needed, and holding the conversational floor. All three are retention devices online.
- **Millennial pause / Gen Z shake**: the half-second of silence before speaking, and the deliberate phone-set-down jolt. Generational markers manufactured from editing habits.
- **Curiosity gap / clickbait / ragebait / trendbait**: four attention instruments. Trendbait is saying something specifically in the hope it becomes a trend ("girl dinner", "Roman Empire", "boomer ellipses").
- **Digital rubbernecking** (Narayanan): engaging with content you dislike, which the feed cannot distinguish from approval.
- **Goodhart's law**: once a metric becomes a target it stops being a good metric.
- **Matthew effect**: small advantages in attention compound into exponential differences in reach.
- **Stated vs revealed preferences**: what people say they want to see vs what they actually engage with. The gap is widening.
- **Poe's law**: sincere extremity reads as satire and satire reads as sincere. A transmission mechanism, not a joke.
- **Online disinhibition effect**: anonymity lowering the cost of hostility.
- **Thought-terminating cliché**: a loaded phrase that closes off dissonant thinking ("it's over", "cope", "AWALT").
- **Flanderization**: progressive self-caricature toward the traits an audience rewards.
- **Enshittification** (Doctorow): platform decay, users first, then business customers, then both.
- **Long tail** (Anderson): selling less of more; the economics microlabels serve.
- **Optimal distinctiveness**: the balance between belonging and being distinct that microlabels exploit.
- **Digital blackface / blaccent**: disproportionate use of Black reaction imagery by non-Black users, and non-Black imitation of African American English.
- **Semantic drift**: ordinary meaning change over time, e.g. "preppy" from prep-school style to bright pink.
- **Backronym**: a false expansion invented after the fact ("gyat" as "girl your ass thick").
- **Doomslang** (Montell): dystopian or dissociated vocabulary — "bedrotting", "doomscrolling", "it's over".
- **Algorithmic anxiety** (Chayka): the felt loss of control online, which Aleksic argues is really anxiety about the emergent effects rather than about the algorithm.
- **Scunthorpe problem**: a filter blocking innocent strings containing banned substrings. The Charles Dickens Museum shadowban is the book's case.
