# Language change: how words survive a ranked feed

## What this shelf is for

One question: given that most new words die, what decides which ones stay. The shelf's answer is that quality has nothing to do with it, and that four measurable properties do — frequency, obtrusiveness, the number of independent contexts a word works in, and whether there was a hole in the language for it to fill.

It is written for cultural analysis that tracks term volume, traces where trends came from, or assigns lexicons to audience segments, all of which need a compiled account of language change.

**It is a one-work shelf, and that is a stated limitation.** Aleksic is a practitioner-linguist writing about a phenomenon three years old; his evidence is mostly his own surveys and his own analytics. The queue that makes this a real shelf is named at the foot of this file. Until those land, read every claim here as a well-argued hypothesis with partial evidence, and prefer the mechanisms to the numbers.

## The unified model: five questions, in order

**1. What need created the word?** Every case in the book starts with an in-group that had a pressing reason to name something and no existing word for it — the mental-health community needing to discuss suicide under moderation, 1980s ball houses naming their own excellence, 4chan incels marking ideological purity, Swifties naming a fan theory. There is no case of a word emerging from the general public. If you cannot name the originating need, you have not found the origin, only an early vehicle.

**2. Is there a semantic gap?** A word with no gap to fill is a fad by construction. The chair emoji is the control: total saturation in September 2021, gone by November, because once the joke ended there was no reason to keep it. Ask what the word does that an existing word does not, and accept "it is funnier" as a real answer — that is the gap "function" filled against "party".

**3. How obtrusive is it?** This is the death predictor and the most useful thing on the shelf. An obtrusive word is one people notice as a word. "Gyat" and "skibidi" are maximally obtrusive; "cancel" and "side-eye" entered the language unnoticed. Obtrusive words survive only as long as they confer in-group status, and that ends the moment the last adopters arrive. The field test: if someone can make fun of you for saying it, it is obtrusive.

**4. How many contexts does it work in?** Count independent phrasal templates and grammatical frames. "Sigma" survived its originating meme because it could be recombined — "what the sigma", "on sigma" — giving it several separate chances to stay in circulation. One template means the word dies with the meme that carried it.

**5. What happens when it leaves the bubble?** Context collapse is the standard end state, not the exception. Once a term reaches audiences the origin community cannot correct, it drifts, gets read in a register nobody intended, and usually turns on the people who made it. Forecast pejoration, not adoption.

## Through-lines

**Quality is not the fitness function; noticeability is.** This shelf's specific contribution to a standing position already carried by [[virality-attention]] is that for words the relevant property is not shareability but invisibility. The words that last are the ones nobody remarks on. Every list of "Gen Z slang" is therefore a list of words that are about to die, and the act of compiling it accelerates the death.

**Suppression moves the word, not the conversation.** This is the correction trend trackers most need. A topic that appears to have gone quiet may simply have changed vocabulary — "suicide" to "unalive" to "un@l!ve" to "sewerslide". A rising substitution rate for a single concept is a leading indicator that the topic is hot and suppressed, and reading it as decline gets the sign backwards. The Whac-A-Mole game is formally unwinnable: flagging every Chinese homophone substitution would false-positive about 20 percent of all posts.

**The origin is almost never the generation using it.** Aleksic's rule of thumb — if a word did not come from 4chan, it probably came from African American English — holds across the book's cases with unnerving regularity. Attribution decays geometrically as a term travels, so by the time a word is "Gen Z slang" its provenance has usually been erased twice over. Do not accept the popular attribution; trace the need.

**Metadata, identity and vocabulary are one object.** A "-core" is a hashtag is a demographic is an aesthetic is a person. TikTok's business page states this directly ("subcultures are the new demographics"). This is the shelf's link to [[status-taste-markets]] and to the standing notion that a classification scheme is a political act — labels multiply not because expression got richer but because targeting got finer, and each label arrives with rules attached.

**Neither humans nor algorithms are the cause.** The book's most disciplined move is to refuse both directions and describe a loop. This matches a Latourian discipline against "the algorithm did it". If you cannot trace the loop — human need, platform incentive, and the return leg — say you cannot, rather than picking a side.

**Irony is not a safe dose.** Poe's law means the joke reading and the sincere reading travel on the same object. The incel-vocabulary pipeline is the worked case: the jokes carried the words to the mainstream, and then a fraction of the audience received them sincerely, and canthal-tilt tutorials and jaw-surgery searches followed. Forecast for both readings.

## Where the book is weakest

Read these as the shelf's known soft spots, and do not lean on them without corroboration.

- **The evidence is thin and author-generated.** The headline numbers come from Aleksic's own convenience surveys of adults reporting on children's speech. That measures adult awareness, which lags usage by an unknown amount, and it cannot separate prevalence from salience.
- **The four survival factors are asserted, not fitted.** They are a good decomposition of Rogers, and the book itself says predicting individual words is pointless. Nothing in the book estimates their relative weights, and nobody should without doing the work.
- **Causation is repeatedly conceded and then used anyway.** Aleksic admits it is "incredibly hard to prove causation" for "no because" and then argues from timing. He is probably right; it is still an argument from coincidence.
- **The offline channel is missing.** Berger's finding that roughly 7 percent of word of mouth happens online is not engaged with. A book about language change measured almost entirely from platform observation inherits that sampling bias.
- **No formal model.** There are no rates, no half-lives, no fitted curves. Any quantitative use of this shelf is the user's own construction and must be labeled as such.

## Kinds of sense change (a stopgap, added 2026-11-02)

Reading what a word's sense does as it crosses from one community to another needs names for the kinds of change. No work on this shelf supplies them yet. Until one does, use the standard terms of historical semantics as Danny Bate's Psyche guide lists them ("How to be a word detective"): **broadening**, **narrowing**, **amelioration**, **pejoration**, **metaphor** and **drift**. Bate adds that prestige and power, as well as nearness, decide which languages lend words, which is the borrowing question this shelf asks of AAE and Gen Z slang.

A measured version: Baes, Haslam and Vylomova (ACL 2024) score semantic change on sentiment, breadth and intensity, so amelioration and pejoration are movements in sentiment and broadening and narrowing are movements in breadth. Label a crossing with these terms; quote a size only once the measure is built.

A second origin for new words, recorded the same day: words a group coins to get past a platform's filters, such as a publisher's own "sex becomes seggs; porn is corn" (Lucy Roeber, It's Nice That, 2026-10-19). Treat these as substitute families, apart from words a group coins for itself.

## The queue

This shelf is one work deep. The named next reads, in priority order:

1. **boyd, *It's Complicated* (2014)** and **Marwick & boyd, context-collapse papers (2011–)**, for exactly this mechanism. Aleksic uses context collapse as a load-bearing term without its primary literature.
2. **McCulloch, *Because Internet* (2019)** and **Crystal, *Language and the Internet* (2001)** — Aleksic names both as covering the previous inflection point. They are the baseline his claims are departures from, and without them "this is new" is untested.
3. **Pinker on the euphemism treadmill** — the source of the shelf's second-most-used concept, currently carried at one remove.
4. **Seaver, *Computing Taste* (2022)**. It supplies the traced chain for recommender systems that this shelf assumes.

Do not treat this shelf as settled until at least the first two land.
