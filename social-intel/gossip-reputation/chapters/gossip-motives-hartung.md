# Better Than Its Reputation?

## The claim

Asked why they gossiped, people name information first and harm last. In an online study
(Frontiers in Psychology 10, 1162; CC BY), 134 adults in Germany each recalled one gossip
conversation at work and one in private and rated six motives for it. Checking what they
already believed about the absent person came first and gathering new information second;
damaging that person's reputation came last. The order held in both settings, and among the
dark-triad traits it was narcissism that moved the motives, while psychopathy moved none. The
authors read the result as gossip's bad reputation failing on the gossipers' own account.

## Core claims

- Six motives underlie gossip in this sample: information validation, information gathering,
  relationship building, protection (warning the listener), social enjoyment and negative
  influence. The study splits Beersma and Van Kleef's (2012) information motive in two and adds
  relationship building.
- Validation ranked first (mean 4.68 on a seven-point scale) and gathering second (3.92).
  Relationship building (3.16), protection (3.10) and social enjoyment (2.83) did not differ
  from one another. Negative influence ranked last (2.14).
- The order held at work and in private. Only social enjoyment moved, higher in private (3.08
  against 2.58), and the authors caution that this depends on the statistical correction used.
- Narcissism predicted validation and relationship building in both settings, gathering and
  social enjoyment at work, and negative influence in private. Machiavellianism predicted
  negative influence at work. Psychopathy predicted nothing; no trait predicted protection.
- Even people high on the dark traits appear to use gossip "to tune their picture of other
  humans and themselves and not to harm others".
- The reading rests on eavesdropping studies (Levin and Arluke 1985; Dunbar et al. 1997) that
  found most overheard talk about others neutral in value.

## Frameworks and models

**The six motives.** A revised Motives to Gossip Questionnaire of eighteen items: two
informational motives (validate, gather), three social (bond, warn, enjoy) and one malicious
(influence negatively).

**Function against intention.** Gossip can be judged by its social functions, which are
consequences a gossiper need not intend, or by the gossiper's intention. The function
literature (Dunbar, Foster, Feinberg) reads gossip positively; the reputation tracks presumed
intention; this study measures intention directly.

**Gossip as low-risk social comparison.** The authors' reading of the narcissism result:
hearing about others is a private way to learn where one stands, without comparing oneself in
public.

**Malice by setting.** Malicious gossip attached to different traits in different places:
Machiavellianism at work, the more competitive setting, and narcissism in private.

## Decision rules

- When reading talk about an absent third party, try the informational reading (checking or
  gathering) before the malicious one, and score talk as hostile only when the text shows an
  intent to damage.
- When an audience agent carries gossip behavior, order its motives validate, gather, then bond,
  warn and enjoy at about equal weight, harm last, unless the room's own evidence says
  otherwise; record that the prior comes from one German convenience sample.
- If a room's gossip reads as mostly hostile, look at the setting before the people: work,
  contests and rank ladders are where malice went with a manipulative trait here.
- Never infer a poster's personality from their gossip. The study runs the other way, and its
  trait measure is a twelve-item screen.
- Treat self-reported motives as what people say they gossip for, and pair them with the
  valence of the talk itself before claiming what gossip in a room does.

## Quantities

Descriptive and regression results from one snowball sample: N = 134, 59% women, mean age
35.25 (SD 13.10). Use the motive order as an ordinal prior and never the means across
populations. Limits stated by the authors: low post hoc power for the setting effect (0.40),
motives taken from the literature and not from the gossipers, one recalled event per setting,
work always asked first, a brief trait measure, a cross-sectional design.

## Key terms

- **Information validation**: gossiping to check one's view of the absent person against the
  listener's.
- **Information gathering**: gossiping to learn something new about the absent person.
- **Protection motive**: gossiping to warn the listener; read by the authors as group
  protection over time.
- **Negative influence**: gossiping to damage the absent person's reputation; endorsed least.
- **Dark triad**: narcissism, Machiavellianism and psychopathy, here measured with the Dirty
  Dozen.
