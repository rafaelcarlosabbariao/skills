# What Makes Online Content Viral?

## The claim

Sharing is driven by physiological arousal, not by whether content feels good or bad. In a census of about 7,000 New York Times articles, high-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) pushed articles onto the most-emailed list while the low-arousal emotion of sadness suppressed sharing, and lab experiments confirmed the arousal mechanism causally. Valence still matters (positive beats negative on average), but arousal is the stronger, more reliable lever.

## Core claims

- Arousal, not valence, is the primary emotional driver of transmission. Activating emotions of either valence (awe, anger, anxiety, amusement) increase sharing; deactivating emotions (sadness, contentment) decrease it.
- Positive content is more viral than negative content on average, once you control for arousal and everything else. Negativity is not a sharing strategy; angry negativity is.
- Awe is a distinct engine: content that opens a sense of vastness and forces a frame update gets shared even when it is long and cognitively demanding.
- Non-emotional utility matters independently: practically useful and surprising or interesting content spreads on its own track, driven by self-presentation and reciprocal exchange, not feeling.
- What tops sharing charts is a biased sample of culture. The most-shared layer over-represents arousing content, so share counts measure activation, not prevalence or agreement.
- The effects survive heavy controls (homepage placement, time of day, section, author fame, length, complexity), so this is content psychology, not just distribution mechanics.

## Frameworks and models

**The arousal-valence grid.** Two orthogonal axes classify any emotional payload: valence (positive to negative) and arousal (activating to deactivating). Sharing loads on the arousal axis. High-arousal positive: awe, amusement, excitement. High-arousal negative: anger, anxiety. Low-arousal positive: contentment. Low-arousal negative: sadness. Predict transmission from the arousal row first, then adjust for valence.

**The NYT field model.** Logistic regression on roughly 7,000 articles (every article on nytimes.com over three months, Aug to Nov 2008), outcome = making the most-emailed list, with coder-rated emotion scores plus controls. Reported effect sizes, per one standard deviation increase in each rated dimension, on the odds of making the list: anger +34%, awe +30%, anxiety +21%, sadness -16%. Practical utility and surprise carried effects of comparable magnitude (utility roughly +30%, surprise around +14%), independent of emotion. Treat these as rank-order anchors, not portable constants.

**The causal arousal experiments.** Holding valence and story content fixed, inducing a high-arousal emotion (amusement rather than contentment, anger or anxiety rather than sadness) raised willingness to share. Arousal is the mechanism, not a correlate. Companion work (Berger 2011) shows even incidental arousal, such as exercise, boosts sharing of unrelated content.

**Two-channel transmission.** Channel one is emotional broadcasting (arousal discharges into sharing). Channel two is social exchange (useful or interesting content shared to help, signal, or bond). A full read of any spreading artifact scores both channels separately.

## Decision rules

- If a topic is spiking on shares or reposts, then ask which arousal emotion is fueling it before treating the spike as a shift in what people believe. Anger-fueled volume is activation, not consensus.
- If content is sad-coded, then expect low transmission regardless of quality; do not read quiet around grief-heavy topics as absence of the feeling.
- If two negative framings compete, then bet on the anger or anxiety framing over the sad framing to dominate circulation.
- If forecasting reach, then score awe potential (vastness plus a forced frame update) as a first-class feature, especially for long or complex material.
- If a piece is useful or genuinely surprising but emotionally flat, then it can still spread; score the exchange channel before writing it off.
- If comparing platforms or metrics, then remember most-shared lists sample arousal. Correct for it before inferring prevalence.

## Key terms

- **Arousal**: physiological activation, the mobilized state some emotions induce; the paper's core predictor of sharing.
- **Valence**: the positive or negative feel of content, a weaker and secondary predictor.
- **Awe**: high-arousal response to vastness that demands mental accommodation; strongly pro-viral.
- **Deactivating emotion**: an emotion (sadness, contentment) that lowers action readiness and suppresses sharing.
- **Practical utility**: usable value in content that drives sharing through the exchange channel, independent of emotion.
- **Most-emailed list**: the NYT sharing chart used as the field outcome; a proxy for transmission, and a biased sample of culture.
- **Social transmission**: person-to-person passing of content, the paper's dependent behavior.
